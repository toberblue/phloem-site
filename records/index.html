Whose record is the essay?

The law has firm answers about records about students, and no answer at all about the writing process — because until now, no school has ever held one. That gap shapes how Phloem enters a school.

The paper trail belongs to the school

State and state-integrated schools in New Zealand are public offices under the Public Records Act 2005, and the records they create about students — school reports, testimonials, test results, assessment grades — are public records. The Privacy Commissioner's education guidance is explicit that the Public Records Act is the primary legislation governing retention and disposal of learner information; the familiar privacy principle about not keeping information longer than needed only applies after the records regime has run its course.

Disposal of those records isn't the school's free choice either. It runs through a disposal authority issued under the Chief Archivist's oversight — for schools, DA 699, expressed in the Ministry's School Records Retention and Disposal Schedule — and destruction needs the school board's permission. This is why we answer the deletion question the way we do: records about students, once they exist, are governed ground, and nobody should promise a student erasure that isn't the school's to give. (Private schools sit outside the Public Records Act; for them the ordinary privacy principles do the work alone.)

The writing was never in the schedule

Here is the part that surprised us, reading the schedule itself: it contains no class for student work at all. Reports about the student, testimonials, results — classified, retained, disposed of by authority. The essay she wrote? Not there. No class for "student work", none for "portfolio", none for "essay". And of course not — schools hand marked work back every day of the school year, and always have. The paper trail is the school's record. The work has always simply been returned to the person who made it.

We flag this because we got it wrong first: an early draft of our own analysis over-read the schedule as covering the work itself, and re-reading the primary source shrank the claim to what it actually says. The correction matters, and it points somewhere more interesting than the overreach did.

The drafting process is new ground

If the handed-in essay was never a school record, the writing of it — the drafts, the revisions, the history of how the work came to be — is even further from one. No school has ever held a student's drafting process, because until recently no such record existed to hold. The exercise book went home in the schoolbag.

A school-provisioned cloud writing tool quietly changes that. The moment a school signs students into a writing platform it administers, every draft and every revision becomes part of the school's information holdings: unclassified ground under the records regime, plus the full privacy machinery for a new category of information about children — collected continuously, at a granularity no exercise book ever had. Records-management guidance already points schools the other way: official records belong in the school's own master systems, not in third-party apps. Nobody has had to rule on any of this, and a school that adopts such a tool is volunteering to be the test case.

Phloem's answer is to keep the question from arising. The garden — the writing and its whole history — stays with the writer; the school provisions nothing and holds nothing new. What the student hands in is what students have always handed in: the finished work, now travelling with its report and sealed record as attachments to the assignment she already submits. Those attachments become school records exactly as her essay always has, on the day she chooses to submit them — and everything before that moment remains what it always was. Hers.
